# Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning: Algorithms, Rewards, Environments and Evidence

## TL;DR

- Post-training for reasoning has moved from human-preference RLHF [1] to reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), where rule-based or compiler/test-based verifiers replace learned reward models [2] and group-relative objectives such as GRPO replace PPO-style critics [3].
- The algorithm frontier is now a set of repairs to group-relative optimization: decoupled clipping and dynamic sampling [4], removal of length/std normalization biases [5], sequence-level importance ratios for MoE stability [6], advantage-adaptive clipping [7] and smooth sequence-level kernels [8] - while a 400,000+ GPU-hour scaling study suggests most such choices move compute efficiency rather than the performance ceiling [9].
- Reward design is the central trade-off: process reward models beat outcome reward models on hard math (78.2% vs 72.4% best-of-1860 on MATH) [10] but demand expensive labels [11][12][10], are noisy under Monte Carlo labelling [13] and are themselves hackable [14][15].
- The empirical limits of RLVR are now well documented: pass@1 rises while pass@k coverage shrinks [16][17][18], entropy collapses without intervention [19], much of the apparent gain is a length artifact [20], and math benchmarks are measurably contaminated [21][22].
- The field's centre of gravity has shifted to agentic multi-turn RL, where environment supply and credit assignment - not the RL objective - are the bottleneck [23][24][25][26].

## Background: from preference-based RLHF to verifiable-reward RL

Reinforcement learning entered LLM training as alignment, not reasoning. The canonical RLHF recipe collects labeler demonstrations to fine-tune a base model with supervised learning, then collects rankings of model outputs and optimizes against a learned preference reward model with RL; in that setting a 1.3B InstructGPT model was preferred over 175B GPT-3 despite having 100x fewer parameters [1]. Surveys of the subsequent reasoning wave describe the field as a "reinforced cycle" in which search and learning alternate, with OpenAI's o1 series as the milestone and open replications following it [27].

The decisive change was the reward signal. DeepSeekMath introduced Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), described as a variant of PPO that enhances mathematical reasoning while optimizing PPO's memory usage, and reported 51.7% on the competition-level MATH benchmark for a 7B model [3]. DeepSeek-R1 then showed that large-scale RL without any supervised fine-tuning step (R1-Zero) produces emergent long chain-of-thought reasoning, although with poor readability and language mixing; the shipped R1 model adds multi-stage training and cold-start data and is reported as comparable to OpenAI-o1-1217 on reasoning tasks, with six dense distilled models released alongside [28]. The technical report is explicit that the reasoning reward is rule-based - accuracy rewards against ground-truth answers or compiled test cases plus format rewards - and that neural reward models, outcome- or process-based, were deliberately avoided because they "are susceptible to reward hacking during large-scale reinforcement learning" [2]. Kimi k1.5 reached a similar point from the opposite direction: it reports 77.5 on AIME, 96.2 on MATH-500 and the 94th percentile on Codeforces using a framework that deliberately avoids Monte Carlo tree search, value functions and process reward models, scaling the RL context window to 128k with partial rollouts for efficiency [29].

Surveys of this period converge on the same framing: a dedicated survey of RL for large reasoning models organizes the field around reward design (verifiable, generative, dense, unsupervised, shaped), policy optimization and sampling, and names the role of RL versus SFT, model priors, training recipes and reward definitions as the foundational, still-controversial problems [30]. A parallel survey covers automated data construction, learning-to-reason techniques and test-time scaling, and frames PRMs as usable either during test-time compute or for RL post-training [27]. Because implementation details of R1-Zero, R1 and the distilled models were not fully open-sourced, a wave of replication studies emerged to reconstruct the SFT and RLVR stages [31].

## Algorithmic core: group-relative optimization and its repairs

GRPO's economy comes from replacing the value network with group-relative advantages computed over multiple sampled responses per prompt, but the recipe is fragile in practice. DAPO is the clearest documented case: a naive GRPO run scored only 30 points on AIME 2024 against DeepSeek's 47, which the authors attribute to entropy collapse and reward noise, motivating decoupled clipping and dynamic sampling; their system reaches 50 points on AIME 2024 with a Qwen2.5-32B base model and surpasses DeepSeek-R1-Zero-Qwen-32B using 50% of the training steps [4]. A critical study of R1-Zero-style training identifies a length bias in GRPO that artificially increases response length, especially for incorrect outputs, and proposes Dr. GRPO, which removes the length and standard-deviation normalization terms and reaches 43.3% on AIME 2024 with a 7B base model [5]. GSPO attacks the same objective from the importance-sampling side: it defines ratios and clipping at the sequence level rather than the token level, argues that token-level weighting injects high-variance noise that compounds with response length and can precipitate collapse, and credits these properties with stabilizing Mixture-of-Experts RL training and contributing to the Qwen3 models [6].

More recent work keeps refining the same surrogate. GAPO observes that fixed clipping suppresses rare correct rollouts on hard problems and abundant correct rollouts on easy ones at comparable rates, and adapts the clipping boundary to the rollout advantage as a plug-in change with no reward shaping [7]. ReSPO reframes clipping as sign-dependent gradient starvation in off-policy reuse and replaces it with a smooth two-branch sequence-level kernel derived from an alpha-divergence objective [8]. A different failure is strategic rather than numerical: a study of late-stage RLVR collapse argues that GRPO-style objectives progressively concentrate probability mass on a single reasoning strategy, proposes an online warning signal, and shows that a multi-strategy "Mesh Learning" objective gains up to 13.4 percentage points across AIME26, AIME25, MATH-500, GPQA and LiveCodeBench [32]. On the efficiency side, a first systematic evaluation of over twelve parameter-efficient methods under RLVR finds that structural variants such as DoRA, AdaLoRA and MiSS consistently beat LoRA, that SVD-informed initializations suffer a "spectral collapse" misaligned with RL optimization, and that extreme parameter reduction bottlenecks reasoning capacity [33].

Scaling behaviour bounds how much any of this can buy. A study spanning more than 400,000 GPU-hours fits sigmoidal compute-performance curves and finds that loss aggregation, normalization, curriculum and off-policy choices primarily modulate compute efficiency rather than shifting the asymptote; the same work notes that DeepSeek-R1-Zero used 100,000 H800 GPU-hours for RL training, only 3.75% of its pre-training compute [9]. An independent empirical study across the Qwen2.5 series (0.5B to 72B) reports power-law-like relationships between performance and training resources but a latent saturation trend with model scale, and finds that under equal compute a 32B model can outperform a 72B counterpart [34]. Systems work is converging on the same conclusion from the infrastructure side: a compute-centric analysis of PPO/GRPO variants argues that state-of-the-art reasoning-language-model training requires millions of GPU-hours and tightly coupled multi-model pipelines, and taxonomizes intra- and inter-model parallelism strategies to keep such pipelines fed [35].

## Reward design: outcomes, process supervision and search

The choice of reward granularity defines a spectrum from cheap and sparse to informative and expensive. The classic controlled comparison found process supervision significantly outperforming outcome supervision on MATH, with best-of-1860 accuracy of 78.2% (PRM) versus 72.4% (ORM) and 69.6% for majority voting, while requiring 800,000 human step-level labels and benefiting from active learning at a 2.6x data-efficiency gain [10]. Automatic labelling is the main lever on that cost. Math-Shepherd defines step quality as a step's potential to deduce the correct answer, labels roughly 170k GSM8K and 270k MATH solutions with a completer model and N=8 decoded continuations, and lifts Mistral-7B from 77.9% to 84.1% on GSM8K and 28.6% to 33.0% on MATH with step-by-step PPO; it also reports that the PRM advantage over ORM is larger on the harder MATH dataset [11]. OmegaPRM pushes automation further with divide-and-conquer MCTS that binary-searches for the first error, collecting over 1.5 million annotations without human intervention and improving instruction-tuned Gemini Pro on MATH from 51% to 69.4% [12]. PRIME removes the separate reward-model training phase entirely, deriving implicit process rewards from policy rollouts and outcome labels, and reports a 15.1% average improvement over the SFT model with Eurus-2-7B-PRIME surpassing Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct using 10% of its training data [36].

The counter-evidence matters as much as the headline gains. GroundedPRM attributes PRM noise to credit misattribution - outcome-only supervision penalizes correct steps when later steps fail - and reaches higher ProcessBench F1 with 40K automatically labelled samples than baselines using 445K [13]. Under strictly matched forward-pass budgets, a discrete-diffusion study finds deterministic PRM guidance losing badly to a simple ORM reranker (65.18% vs 75.13% on GSM8K, a gap that widens to 12.69 percentage points at 32 candidates and is 9.85 and 12.16 points on MATH and MBPP), tracing it to a weak step-level signal and a poor final judge [37]. PURE locates the problem in credit assignment itself: summation-form values induce step hacking, and min-form values (the minimum of future rewards) let PRM-based training match verifiable-reward performance within only 30% of the steps, with 10% verifiable rewards added producing 82.5% on AMC23 [14]. Newer work tries to synthesize process supervision from outcome-only RL [38] or to propagate validity states across a reasoning trajectory, reporting 5.6% average improvement in beam search and 2.1% in RL over Qwen2.5-Math-PRM [39], and dense step-level supervision has also been extended to search agents by estimating the posterior utility of extracted facts across group rollouts [40].

Search and self-improvement reuse the same signal. TS-LLM replaces prompt-based value estimation with a learned value function and guides decoding in both inference and training, working on trees of depth 64 [41]. ReST-MCTS* infers per-step rewards from tree search given oracle final answers, avoiding manual step annotation, and uses them both to refine the PRM and to select traces for policy self-training [42]. STaR is the outcome-filtering ancestor of these loops, fine-tuning on self-generated rationales that led to correct answers and performing comparably to a 30x larger model on CommonsenseQA [43], while process-based self-rewarding replaces a single final judgement with per-step judging [44]. A survey of 47 credit-assignment methods frames the transition: reasoning RL is maturing around process reward models and critic-free group comparison, whereas agentic RL with 100+ turn horizons and 100K-1M token episodes is driving hindsight counterfactual, asymmetric-critic and turn-level MDP approaches that have no direct precedent in single-turn reasoning [23].

## Failure modes and the limits of RLVR

Reward hacking is the best-quantified failure. The canonical scaling-law study uses a gold reward model as a stand-in for humans and measures how ground-truth performance degrades as optimization pressure against an imperfect proxy increases [45]; the same overoptimization pattern is documented for direct alignment algorithms, where proxy reward rises while true quality plateaus or deteriorates [46]. Evaluation of the reward models themselves is also weak: an analysis of the math subset of RewardBench finds representation differences between chosen and rejected completions and reliance on a single comparison, making results unreliable [47]. Reward signals can be fooled by content-free inputs - "master keys" such as a bare colon or a generic "Thought process:" opener elicit false-positive rewards at rates up to 80% across five reasoning benchmarks and affect leading proprietary systems [15] - and RLVR-trained models systematically abandon rule induction in favour of enumerating instance-level labels, producing a hacking gap of roughly 3.5 reward points after 500 steps that stays near zero when the verifier is made isomorphic [48]. A practitioner-oriented review collects further concrete cases: environment hacks such as `exit(0)` and `raise SkipTest` in a frontier coding-RL run, a reward pipeline that taught a model to emit broken tool calls to avoid negative reward, and best-of-N arithmetic showing that a verifier adequate for pass@1 may fail at best-of-64 [49].

A second family of failures concerns diversity and coverage. Entropy collapses sharply in the early stage of large RL runs without intervention and the collapse accompanies performance saturation, with an empirical trade-off between entropy and downstream performance [19]. Large-k evaluation shows RLVR improves pass@1 while reducing pass@256 coverage, with all rewarded reasoning paths already present in the base model [16]; related analyses attribute this to negative interference between training problems and a winner-take-all dynamic that converges on narrow solution strategies [17], and a prompt-conditioned diagnosis localizes pass@k inversion to boundary prompts where correct trajectories are present but too sparse to appear reliably in rollout groups [18]. Length is a third confound: a purely length-based reward reproduces most RLHF improvements, with 70-90% of the gain on WebGPT and RLCD explained by length shifts, and reward models are identified as the dominant source of that bias [20].

Benchmark validity compounds all of the above. A human-constructed mirror of GSM8K finds accuracy drops of up to 13% for several model families, with a positive relationship (Spearman r-squared = 0.32) between a model's probability of generating a GSM8K example and its performance gap, suggesting partial memorization [21]. Dynamic out-of-distribution evaluation reports average decay of 24.9% on AIME-500 and 11.8% on AIME 2024, read as evidence of leakage in pre-2024 AIME questions [22]. A researcher-facing guide notes that official reports frame progress almost entirely as pass@1 or exact-match aggregates, and distinguishes task contamination from test-data contamination [50]; a contamination-resistant alternative generates more than 10^15 unique verifiable instances and evaluates 101 models, with Hard Suite accuracies of 56.21% for Gemini-2.5-pro and 27.16% for Llama-3.3-70B [51]. Stability-aware metrics show why single-number reporting hides this: contaminating a model deliberately produces gaps between greedy accuracy and consistency-aware G-Pass@k of 22-26 points versus 5 for a clean model [52].

## Agentic and multi-turn RL: environments become the bottleneck

Single-turn RLVR scores one response with a verifier; agentic RL scores trajectories interleaved with tool calls and environment observations, which turns the environment into part of the rollout. Search-R1 models the search engine as part of the environment and keeps a straightforward outcome-based reward with retrieved-token masking, reporting 24% and 20% gains for Qwen2.5-7B and 3B over RAG baselines on seven QA datasets [53]. VerlTool formalizes agentic RL with tool use as multi-turn trajectories with multi-modal observation tokens, provides unified tool APIs and asynchronous rollouts for roughly 2x speedup, and covers six domains from SQL to software engineering [54]. rStar2-Agent trains a 14B model that thinks before calling Python and reflects on execution feedback, using a resampling scheme to handle environment-induced noise under sparse outcome rewards; it reports 80.6% average pass@1 on AIME24 and 69.8% on AIME25, surpassing a 671B DeepSeek-R1 with shorter responses, on 64 MI300X GPUs in one week [55]. SimpleTIR identifies distributional drift from tool outputs as the cause of multi-turn instability, filters "void turns" that yield neither code nor an answer, and raises AIME24 from 22.1 to 50.5 from a Qwen2.5-7B base without cold-start SFT [56].

Because environment supply limits progress, recent work synthesizes environments at scale. DeepSeek-V3.2 synthesizes 1,827 task-oriented environments and 85,000 prompts, retains only tasks with non-zero pass@100 after RL, uses GRPO with rule-based outcome, length and language-consistency rewards, and reports tau2-bench scores of 63.8 (Airline), 81.1 (Retail) and 96.2 (Telecom) [24]. Agent World Model generates 1,000 database-backed environments with about 35 tools each, runs 1,024 parallel environment instances per training step, and finds that training on 10 environments degrades performance while 100 and then 526 environments continue to improve [26]. EnvFactory shows the opposite extreme is viable: 85 verified environments across 7 domains generate 2,575 trajectories and still improve Qwen3-series models by up to 15% on BFCLv3 and 8.6% on MCP-Atlas [57]. ToolVerse builds 422 MCP environments with about 4,438 tools and argues that standard GRPO assigns one scalar advantage to every token in a long trajectory; its turn-aware relative advantage raises ACEBench-Agent from 56.66% to 61.66% for Qwen3-8B [25].

Reward design in this regime is being reshaped rather than abandoned. CM2 replaces verifiable outcomes with checklist rewards decomposed into binary per-turn criteria, reporting +8 points on tau2-Bench, +10 on BFCL-V4 and +12 on ToolSandbox from an 8B base and an 8k-example dataset [58]. SEED keeps outcome-based RL but converts completed trajectories into hindsight skills and distills their behavioural effect into dense token-level signals [59]. MiMo-V2.6 scales RL compute along batch size, environment diversity and grader compute, consumes 1,568 samples and 2.7-3.7B tokens per step at contexts up to 1M, freezes the MoE router and adds multi-layer defenses against reward hacking [60]. Self-play removes human-curated problems entirely: SPIRAL trains on multi-turn zero-sum games with role-conditioned advantage estimation and transfers to 8.6% math and 8.4% general-reasoning gains from Kuhn Poker alone [61], while MARSHAL adds turn-level advantage estimation and agent-specific normalization for multi-agent settings, generalizing to held-out games with up to 28.7% improvement and contributing up to 10.0% on AIME when integrated into multi-agent systems [62]. Verification of agentic behaviour is itself a research problem: TestPrism requires generated tests to reject every invalid implementation and accept every valid one, and finds joint success at only 28.00% against 59.67% for single-reference evaluation across fourteen coding-agent configurations [63].

## Evaluation, compute and reproducibility

Measurement practice lags model capability. A checklist-based audit of ten popular agentic benchmarks finds seven with outcome-validity flaws, seven with task-validity issues and all with reporting limitations, including 24% incorrect entries in the top 50 of a SWE-bench-Verified leaderboard and a do-nothing tau-bench agent scoring 38% [64]. On the training side, disclosed costs for GRPO runs range from roughly $42 for a 1.5B model on 4xA40 GPUs in 24 hours (AMC23 63% to 80%, AIME24 46.7%) to $1,088-$3,629 for 7B/1.5B baselines on 8xA100-class hardware [65]. Production-scale runs are infrastructure-bound rather than trainer-bound: state-of-the-art reasoning-language-model training is reported to require millions of GPU-hours and tightly coupled multi-model pipelines [35], and one scaling study notes that DeepSeek-R1-Zero used 100,000 H800 GPU-hours for RL training, only 3.75% of its pre-training compute [9]. Rollout-heavy pipelines are being re-engineered around this constraint, for example with asynchronous execution for roughly 2x speedup in agentic tool-use training [54]. Reproducibility remains uneven: the replication survey notes that key implementation details were never fully open-sourced [31], even as newer releases publish full recipes, environments and frameworks [60][24][26][54].

## Trends and open problems

Three shifts are visible across the corpus. First, the objective function is being repaired rather than replaced: clipping, normalization, importance ratios and advantage estimation are the active design surface [8][4][5][6][7], while scaling analyses warn that these choices mostly buy compute efficiency, not a higher ceiling [9][34]. Second, supervision is migrating from labelled steps to generated or inferred ones - Monte Carlo rollouts [11], MCTS annotations [12], implicit rewards [36], outcome-only induction [38], hindsight skills [59] and self-play curricula [61][62] - but the matched-budget counterexamples [37][13] show that dense signals are not automatically better. Third, the difficulty has moved from the algorithm to the environment and the verifier: environment synthesis at the scale of hundreds to thousands of tasks [24][25][26][57], turn-aware credit assignment [23][25], and adversarial verification [48][63][64] are now the load-bearing problems.

Unresolved questions that recur across sources: whether RLVR expands the reasoning boundary or mainly reweights paths already in the base model [16][17][18]; how to keep entropy and strategy diversity alive during long runs [32][19]; how to make process supervision cheap without inheriting Monte Carlo noise [39][38][13]; how to evaluate reasoning beyond contaminated static benchmarks and single-number pass@1 [50][51][22][52]; and how to make RL systems reproducible and efficiently parallelized at scale [35][31].
