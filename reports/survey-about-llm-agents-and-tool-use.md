# LLM Agents and Tool Use: Architectures, Tool Learning, Evaluation and Open Problems

## TL;DR

- Agentic tool use grew out of a two-step shift: chain-of-thought prompting showed that step-by-step reasoning is an emergent ability of large models [1], and ReAct showed that interleaving such reasoning traces with environment actions beats either acting or reasoning alone [2].
- Today, tool use is mostly a data-and-retrieval problem: self-supervised API filtering [3], instruction tuning on verified synthetic calls [4][5], and dense retrieval over API pools of thousands of tools [6][7] — yet, as reported in the papers' own evaluations, test-time retriever quality can dominate final accuracy, degrading it by 29% to 52% relative to a ground-truth retriever [8].
- Realistic interactive evaluation still shows huge gaps: 14.41% vs 78.24% human success on WebArena [9], 15% vs 92% human on GAIA [10], and reliability rather than best-of-k capability is the binding constraint (pass^8 below 25% on tau-bench retail) [11].
- Benchmarking itself is fragile: an audit of four tool-calling benchmark families measured an 18.5% evaluator-human misalignment rate and an 18.9-percentage-point spread across 23 repeated runs of the same configuration [12].
- The frontier has moved to interfaces, scale and security: tailored agent-computer interfaces lift SWE-bench resolution from 3.8% to 12.47% [13], standardized tool plumbing arrived with MCP [14], routing degrades 16-23 F1 points when catalogs grow to hundreds of tools [15], and tool poisoning succeeds against o1-mini 72.8% of the time [16].

## Background: what an "LLM agent" is, and why tools matter

The term is used loosely, so it is worth fixing definitions. A widely used survey defines AI agents as artificial entities that sense their environment, make decisions, and take actions, and offers a general LLM-agent framework with three components — brain, perception, and action — where the brain (an LLM) stores memory and knowledge and performs reasoning, planning and decision-making, the perception module extends sensing beyond text to sounds, visuals, touch and smell, and the action module covers textual output, embodied action and tool use [17]. A complementary survey organizes the same design space around a profiling module, a memory module, a planning module and an action module, and explicitly lists external tools as a way to execute actions in domains requiring expert knowledge and to mitigate hallucination that LLMs cannot resolve internally [18].

What makes tool use conceptually distinct is that it expands the model's competence without touching its weights. The Augmented Language Models survey frames augmentation along two axes — reasoning, defined as decomposing a complex task into simpler subtasks, and tool use, defined as calling external modules such as a code interpreter — and stresses that these augmentations can be combined and can be either heuristic or learned from demonstrations while still following the standard missing-token-prediction objective [19]. An early systems paper made the architectural case first: MRKL argues that knowledge and reasoning are separate problems from linguistic processing, and proposes a neuro-symbolic architecture combining multiple neural models with discrete knowledge and reasoning modules, implemented as Jurassic-X [20]. The tool-learning literature then generalized this into a framework with a controller (typically a foundation model), a tool set, an environment, a perceiver and a human, in which the model decomposes an instruction into subtasks, adjusts its plan through reasoning, and conquers each subtask by selecting an appropriate tool, trained either from demonstrations or from feedback [21].

The enabling ingredient was reasoning capability at scale. Chain-of-thought prompting supplies exemplars of the form `<input, chain of thought, output>`, requires no finetuning, and is an emergent ability: it does not help small models and yields gains only at roughly 100B+ parameters, with prompting PaLM 540B with eight exemplars reaching state-of-the-art accuracy on GSM8K and 75.6% on StrategyQA versus a prior 69.4% [1]. Reasoning traces alone, however, cannot ground the model in the world — which is precisely the gap the next section addresses.

## Reasoning and acting: the core agent loop

ReAct is the canonical interface between reasoning and tool use. Instead of generating a hidden chain of thought and then acting, the model emits reasoning traces and task-specific actions interleaved in a single stream, so that traces help it induce, track and update plans and handle exceptions while actions let it query external sources such as a Wikipedia API; the paradigm is prompt-only, needing one or two in-context examples [2]. The evidence is mixed in an instructive way: on knowledge-intensive QA, ReAct reaches 27.4 exact match on HotpotQA versus 33.4 for self-consistency CoT and only 25.7 for act-only prompting, though combining ReAct with CoT self-consistency reaches 35.1 [2]. The advantage appears where interaction with an environment matters: the paper reports that ReAct, prompted with only one or two in-context examples, outperforms imitation and reinforcement-learning methods by an absolute success rate of 34% on ALFWorld and 10% on WebShop [2].

The paper is also candid about the limits, and those limits foreshadow the rest of the field: performance remains far from expert humans, prompting gives only limited support for reasoning and acting behaviours, and large action spaces need more demonstrations than fit in the context window [2]. Its failure analysis attributes errors to hallucinated reasoning traces and facts plus search-result errors [2]. Later surveys absorb the same framing — reasoning as task decomposition and tool use as calling external modules [19] — and the difference between ReAct-style prompted agents and weight-level tool learning became the field's main empirical axis.

## Teaching models to call tools: self-supervision, instruction data and native function calling

Toolformer attacks the problem without human supervision: a model is trained to decide which APIs to call, when, with what arguments, and how to incorporate results into future token prediction, using only a handful of demonstrations per API [3]. The training signal is a perplexity filter — sampled calls are kept when they reduce perplexity on future tokens, and the model is finetuned on the retained calls — and the resulting 6.7B GPT-J model outperforms a much larger GPT-3 on a range of downstream tasks without sacrificing language modelling ability [3]. Its tool set is small and generic (calculator, QA system, search engines, translation, calendar), which is the natural ceiling of the method: it scales with API quality and demonstration availability, not with catalog size [3].

The instruction-tuning branch instead treats tool calling as a supervised generation task over large API corpora. Gorilla finetunes LLaMA-7B to write API calls and surpasses GPT-4 on that task, evaluated on APIBench — 94 exhaustive TorchHub calls, 696 TensorHub calls and 925 most-downloaded HuggingFace models, with ten Self-Instruct prompts per API [8]. In the paper's reported evaluation the zero-shot overall APIBench accuracy and hallucination rate are 59.13/6.98 for Gorilla on TorchHub against 38.70/36.55 for GPT-4, and 83.79/5.40 versus 18.20/78.65 on TensorHub [8]. The paper's own diagnosis is that prompting frontier models produces "dire hallucination errors" such as inventing repository names [8].

ToolLLM scales the same recipe to 16,464 real RESTful APIs across 49 categories from RapidAPI Hub, generated and annotated with ChatGPT, and adds DFSDT, a depth-first search over decision trees that replaces linear ReAct trajectories: ChatGPT+DFSDT averages a 63.8 pass rate versus 35.3 for ReAct and 44.5 for a cost-matched ReAct@N [6]. Finetuned ToolLLaMA with DFSDT reaches 66.7 pass / 60.0 win, comparable to its ChatGPT teacher, while Vicuna and Alpaca score zero on the same benchmark [6]. The reported reliability of the automatic evaluator, ToolEval, is 87.1% human agreement on pass rate and 80.3% on win rate [6]. DFSDT is explicitly costlier in API calls than ReAct, and manual API curation at this scale is impractical — which motivates the retrieval discussion below [6].

The most recent wave attacks data quality rather than model scale. APIGen collects 3,673 executable APIs across 21 categories and verifies every generated point in three hierarchical stages (format check, real function execution, semantic verification), yielding a 60,000-entry dataset on which a 7B model reaches state-of-the-art on the Berkeley Function-Calling Benchmark, outperforming several GPT-4 models, while a 1B model surpasses GPT-3.5-Turbo [4]. ToolACE instead runs a self-evolution synthesis process to curate 26,507 APIs, generates dialogs through multiple interacting agents guided by a complexity evaluator, and enforces accuracy with dual-layer rule-based and model-based verification, producing 8B models whose performance is described as comparable to contemporary GPT-4 [5]. Both papers frame prior datasets as static and under-verified [4][5]. On the training-objective side, Granite-20B-FunctionCalling trains a single model on seven fundamental function-calling tasks — nested calls, chaining, parallel functions, function-name detection, parameter-value detection, next-best function and response generation — and reports the best open-model and fourth overall result on the Berkeley Function Calling Leaderboard with better generalizability across seven evaluation datasets [22]. Finally, data need not come from the open web: Synthesis Through Simulation generates schema-free tool-calling data by having an agent execute operations against policy-enforcing APIs in simulated enterprise environments, so validity holds by construction; its Generalist Populator reaches 0.88 average marginal fidelity and 100% constraint satisfaction across ten environments without database schemas, whereas schema-privileged agents fail 82% of trajectories on the tightly coupled airline workflows [23].

Taken together, this branch shows a clear trajectory: from prompt-level control [2], to self-supervised demonstration-free learning [3], to massive instruction corpora [6], to automated verification of synthetic data [4][5][23]. What none of it removes is the need to pick the right tool at inference time.

## Retrieval and selection over large tool catalogs

Once the tool set exceeds a few hundred entries, selection becomes the bottleneck, and the literature repeatedly finds that the retriever — not the model — determines the outcome. Gorilla's retriever-aware training adds 12.37% accuracy on TorchHub and 23.46% on HuggingFace relative to training without retrieval, but the paper reports that at evaluation time a GPT-Index retriever causes 29.20% accuracy degradation and BM25 causes 52.27% degradation relative to a ground-truth retriever; the authors warn that a non-optimal test-time retriever misguides the model into more errors [8]. ToolLLM answers this with a Sentence-BERT dense retriever trained contrastively on positive and negative APIs; feeding its top-5 APIs to ToolLLaMA-DFSDT yields 67.3 pass / 63.1 win, which the authors argue even exceeds the ground-truth API set by enlarging the relevant-API search space [6].

Work explicitly framed as massive tool retrieval targets the low-resource regime. MTRB provides three subsets of 90 test and 10 training samples with refined tool descriptions, and the query-tool alignment framework rewrites user queries via ranking functions and direct preference optimization, improving top-5 and top-10 retrieval by up to 93.28% on Sufficiency@k, with up to 78.53% improvement from a single annotated sample and strong cross-dataset generalizability [7]. The counterweight is a caution about what these benchmarks actually measure. ToolSense shows that parametric tool retrievers, which encode each tool as a virtual token and report over 90% recall on standard ToolBench retrieval benchmarks, collapse by roughly 50-64 percentage points on its realistic retrieval benchmark with ambiguity tiers, falling below an embedding-model baseline, and exhibit a "knowledge-retrieval dissociation" in which strong retrievers score near-random on factual probes; the authors attribute this to verbose, fully-specified benchmark queries combined with constrained trie decoding [24].

The same effect appears at production scale. A controlled study on a catalog of 110 agents and 584 tools from a deployed enterprise assistant finds that routing F1 on under-specified requests drops 16-23 percentage points across models as the catalog grows from 10 to 110 agents; an oracle analysis splits the loss into a 16-point retrieval gap and a confusion gap that lowers the oracle ceiling from 79% to 69% [15]. Embedding-based shortlisting with k=20 recovers 10-11 F1 points at full scale across three models and two providers, confirmed by a 1,435-utterance three-annotator production study showing 10-17 points on real traffic, while shrinking the routing prompt from 584 to 20 tool definitions at roughly 50 ms (API) or 2 ms (local) added latency [15]. Notably, the study's shortlisting comparison finds BM25 falling below flat routing at every scale point because enterprise tools share vocabulary, and reports that a fine-tuned 335M retriever reaches 48.7% versus 52.5% for text-embedding-3-large [15]. Platform-level responses are already visible — namespace-based tool search from OpenAI, BM25 retrieval over tool descriptions from Anthropic, and growing MCP server registries [15].

## Evaluating tool-using agents: interactive environments and their fragility

Agent evaluation splits into interactive environments and tool-call correctness suites, and both report large gaps. WebArena builds four self-hosted, fully functional applications plus utility tools and knowledge bases, and evaluates outcome-based functional correctness on 812 long-horizon tasks: the best GPT-4 agent reaches 14.41% end-to-end success versus 78.24% for humans, and GPT-4 wrongly declares 54.9% of feasible tasks impossible [9]. Its failure taxonomy — observation bias toward the first plausible information, inability to reinterpret observations and recover, and lack of exploration — recurs throughout the field [9]. AgentBench evaluates 29 API-based and open models across 8 interactive environments and attributes the main obstacles to poor long-term reasoning, poor decision-making and weak instruction following, adding that multi-round alignment data helps while training on code has an ambivalent effect [25]. GAIA targets general assistants with 466 questions requiring reasoning, multimodality, browsing and tool use: humans score 92% versus 15% for GPT-4 with plugins, with 300 answers retained for the public leaderboard and Level 3 questions demanding multi-hop tool use across sources [10].

Because real API status changes between runs, StableToolBench interposes a virtual API server with a caching system and API simulators, and replaces unstable metrics with GPT-4-evaluated "solvable pass and win rates" specifically to remove evaluation randomness [26]. A different instability is measured by tau-bench, which simulates a user conversing with an agent that has domain APIs and policies, compares final database state against annotated goals, and introduces pass^k — the probability that all k i.i.d. trials succeed — as a consistency metric: the paper finds that state-of-the-art function-calling agents such as gpt-4o succeed on fewer than 50% of tasks and are quite inconsistent, with pass^8 below 25% in the retail domain [11]. Its analysis additionally reports hallucinated argument IDs at 0.46 per task for gpt-4o versus 2.08 and 6.34 for gpt-3.5-turbo variants, and an ablation in which removing the domain policy from the prompt costs 4.4% pass^1 in retail but 22.4% in airline — evidence that success often comes from intuitive tool use rather than policy adherence; the agent-plus-simulator setup costs roughly $0.38 + $0.23 per retail task, about $200 for a single trial over the suite [11].

Worst of all, the measurement apparatus itself is questionable. A validity audit of four benchmark families — BFCL v4, tau2-Bench, LiveMCPBench and MCP-Atlas — finds 92 evaluator-human disagreements across 496 expert-reviewed tasks, an 18.5% misalignment rate, and reports that 23 repeated evaluations of the same LiveMCPBench setup produced scores from 57.9% to 76.8%, an 18.9-point spread large enough to change leaderboard conclusions [12]. It catalogues failure mechanisms on both sides: brittle state matching, trajectory lock-in, incorrect ground truths, substring-based communication failures and reward-basis misalignment for deterministic benchmarks, and rubric drift, hallucinated completion and answer-only scoring for LLM judges, concluding that reported scores can reflect evaluator artifacts rather than agent capability and calling for decomposed metrics that separate tool invocation, task completion and outcome verification [12].

## Interfaces, protocols and multi-agent orchestration

A parallel line of work argues that the interface, not the model, is the lever. SWE-agent introduces the agent-computer interface as an abstraction layer between the LM agent and the computer, on the thesis that interfaces tailored for language models outperform human-oriented interfaces such as the Linux shell; with GPT-4 Turbo it resolves 12.47% (286/2,294) of the full SWE-bench test set and 18.00% (54/300) of SWE-bench Lite against a previous best non-interactive retrieval-augmented rate of 3.8%, a 64% relative increase over a shell-only baseline, and reaches 87.7% pass@1 on HumanEvalFix [13]. The trade-off is explicit: SWE-agent is 8-13x more costly than RAG on Lite for a 6.7-fold improvement in resolved rate, successful runs finish at a median of $1.21 and 12 steps versus $2.52 and 21 steps for failures, and editing remains weak — 51.7% of instances have trajectories with at least one failed edit, and edit-attempt success drops from 90.5% overall to 57.2% after a single failure [13].

Standardization arrived on 2024-11-25, when Anthropic open-sourced the Model Context Protocol as a single connection standard, shipping the specification and SDKs, local server support in Claude Desktop, and an open-source repository of servers for Google Drive, Slack, GitHub, Git, Postgres and Puppeteer, with Block, Apollo, Zed, Replit, Codeium and Sourcegraph as early adopters [14]. The protocol's own specification treats tools as arbitrary code execution that must be handled with caution, treats tool descriptions and annotations as untrusted unless the server is trusted, and requires hosts to obtain explicit user consent before invoking a tool [14].

Multi-agent orchestration, by contrast, remains poorly characterized. MAFBench argues that the architectural structures imposed by multi-agent frameworks govern how agents interact, store information and coordinate tasks, yet their impact on performance is poorly understood, and reports that framework-level design choices alone can increase latency by over 100x, reduce planning accuracy by up to 30%, and lower coordination success from above 90% to below 30%; it contributes an architectural taxonomy plus a unified evaluation suite covering orchestration overhead, memory behaviour, planning, specialization and coordination [27]. A complementary empirical study of failures collects over 1,600 annotated traces across 7 popular multi-agent frameworks and derives a taxonomy of 14 failure modes in 3 categories — system design issues, inter-agent misalignment and task verification — validated on 150 traces with expert annotators at kappa = 0.88, with an LLM-as-a-Judge pipeline to scale annotation [28]. The two results are mutually reinforcing: coordination gains are real but brittle, and most failures are organizational rather than per-model capability failures [28][27].

## Long-horizon memory and safety of tool-using agents

Long-horizon tasks expose context management as an unsolved systems problem. UltraHorizon evaluates exploration-based tasks with trajectories averaging 200k+ tokens and 400+ tool calls in the heaviest setting and more than 35k tokens and 60 tool calls in standard configuration, and finds LLM agents consistently underperform human participants while "simple scaling fails"; trajectory analysis isolates eight error types attributed to in-context locking and functional capability gaps [29]. REMORY responds at the representation level by supplementing a textual summary with a bounded sequence of soft memory tokens trained so that a frozen LLM can approximate the continuation it would produce with the full history, improving source attribution on SummHay at nearly unchanged insight coverage while approaching full-context performance using only 5.2% of input positions; on long-horizon agent benchmarks it reports fewer repeated tool outputs and tool errors on BrowseComp and Terminal-Bench 2.1 [30]. Both results point the same way: context budget, not raw reasoning depth, is often the limiting resource [30][29].

Safety in tool-using agents is qualitatively different from content safety, because the harm results from legitimate tools being invoked with malicious parameters. Agent-SafetyBench spans 349 interaction environments and 2,000 test cases across 8 risk categories and 10 failure modes, and none of the 16 evaluated agents exceeds a 60% safety score; the authors summarize two fundamental defects — lack of robustness and lack of risk awareness — and conclude that defence prompts alone are likely insufficient [31]. MCPTox demonstrates the concrete attack surface: with the first benchmark for tool poisoning, where malicious instructions are embedded in tool metadata without execution, built on 45 live MCP servers and 353 authentic tools with 1,312 malicious cases, o1-mini shows a 72.8% attack success rate, ASR exceeds 60% for GPT-4o mini, o1-mini, DeepSeek-R1 and Phi-4, and refusal is rare, with the highest refusal rate (Claude-3.7-Sonnet) below 3% [16]. The poisoned tool is never executed — all malicious actions are performed by legitimate tools on the server — and re-purposing indirect prompt-injection payloads as tool descriptions yields near-0% success, so tool poisoning needs its own methodology [16]. More capable models were often more susceptible because the attack exploits instruction following, and enabling reasoning mode for Qwen3 raised its average ASR by 27.8% [16]. A recent survey of tool-use methods and challenges converges on the same list of unresolved deployment blockers: knowledge conflicts between internal priors and external evidence, performance degradation in long-context scenarios, non-monotonic scaling behaviour in compound systems, and novel security vulnerabilities [32].

## Trends and open problems

Three shifts are visible across the material surveyed here. First, capability moved from prompting to data engineering: from one or two ReAct exemplars [2] and self-supervised API filtering with a perplexity criterion [3], through 16,464-API corpora annotated by a teacher model [6], to automated multi-stage verification of synthetic calls [4][5] and simulation-based generation inside enterprise constraints [23]. Second, the interface and protocol layer became a first-class research object, with tailored agent-computer interfaces producing large gains without any weight change [13] and MCP standardizing how tools are exposed and consented to [14]. Third, the failure modes that dominate the current literature are systemic rather than purely cognitive: retrieval and routing degrade as catalogs scale [15], measurement noise rivals model differences [12], coordination breaks at the framework level [28][27], context compaction and long-horizon planning remain unsolved [30][29], and legitimate tool channels are exploitable [31][16].

Several concrete gaps follow directly from the evidence. Reliable evaluation is the most urgent: with 18.5% evaluator-human misalignment and 18.9-point repeat-run spread on a tool-calling benchmark [12], and with consistency metrics such as pass^8 collapsing far below pass^1 [11], many reported improvements may not be distinguishable from noise, especially given that prompting details alone move WebArena scores by several points [9]. Tool retrieval is a second gap that is better characterized than solved: benchmark-strong parametric retrievers can collapse by 50-64 points on realistic ambiguous queries [24], and production-scale routing loses 16-23 F1 points as the catalog grows [15]. Third, self-reported limitations cluster around three items: hallucinated APIs and brittle test-time retrieval in tool-calling models [8][6], static and under-verified datasets in the synthetic-data literature [4][5][23], and safety and trustworthiness of tool use as an open problem named by early framings [21] and only partially addressed by current defences [31][16]. Finally, the survey literature explicitly lists knowledge conflicts between parametric priors and retrieved evidence, long-context degradation, and non-monotonic scaling in compound systems as unsolved [32] — the same phenomena that UltraHorizon measures as in-context locking [29] and that MAFBench measures as framework-induced latency and coordination collapse [27]. The most plausible near-term research agenda is therefore decomposed, stability-aware evaluation [12], retrieval and routing that is tested on ambiguous rather than fully-specified queries [24][15], and memory architectures that make long-horizon context compaction faithful rather than merely shorter [30].
