# Video and Multimodal Generation: Architectures, Systems, Evaluation and Open Problems

## TL;DR

- Modern video generation has converged on a two-stage latent recipe — a spatio-temporal tokenizer (3D VAE or discrete VQ) followed by a Transformer denoiser trained with diffusion or flow matching — after the video 3D U-Net of the first diffusion systems was displaced by the Diffusion Transformer [1][2][3][4].
- Open-weight video models now match the frontier recipe and compete on scale and serving cost: Wan 2.1 ships 1.3B/14B variants with the small model needing only 8.19 GB VRAM [5], HunyuanVideo trains a 13B flow-matching model [6], and Wan 2.2 replaces one denoiser with a two-expert mixture-of-experts of 27B total but 14B active parameters [7].
- Unified multimodal generation is an unresolved architectural debate: discrete-token autoregression [8][9], next-token prediction plus diffusion in one Transformer [10][11], autoregressive backbones with flow-matching heads over continuous tokens [12], MMDiT-style dual-weight attention [13], and fully any-to-any discrete-flow or masked-diffusion models [14][15][16][17].
- Evaluation is the weakest link: model reports lean on vendor-internal human-preference studies [5][6], public leaderboards rank models by pairwise Elo-style voting [18], and benchmarks have moved from perceptual fidelity toward intrinsic faithfulness [19] and physical validity, where a benchmark scoring videos against physical principles caps at 100% while the strongest verified entry in the retrieved snapshot reaches only 58.2% [20].
- Efficiency and long-horizon consistency stay open: video-specific acceleration work is still a minority of the acceleration literature [21], distribution distillation is reported as the only branch that reliably reaches the one-to-four-step regime but is described as "fragile" [21], and streaming memory cost still grows without bound [22][23].

## Background

"Video and multimodal generation" covers models that synthesise temporally consistent image sequences and the models that generate several modalities — text, image, audio, video — inside one system. The first modern recipe was discrete: VideoGPT learned downsampled discrete video latents with a VQ-VAE built from 3D convolutions and axial self-attention, then modelled them with a GPT-style autoregressive transformer using spatio-temporal position encodings, producing samples competitive with GANs on BAIR and high-fidelity natural videos on UCF-101 and TGIF [24]. The alternative lineage replaced likelihood over discrete tokens with iterative denoising: Video Diffusion Models applied a 3D U-Net diffusion architecture to a fixed number of frames, showed that joint training on images and video reduces the variance of minibatch gradients, and introduced a conditional sampling scheme for temporal and spatial extension that yields longer and higher-resolution videos [1]. Imagen Video then demonstrated that this recipe scales into a text-conditional system by cascading a base video model with interleaved spatial and temporal super-resolution models, using v-parameterization and progressive distillation to reach 1280 x 768, 5.3 seconds at 24 frames per second [2].

The pivot that shaped everything after it was architectural rather than objective-related: the Diffusion Transformer (DiT) replaced the U-Net backbone with a transformer operating on latent patches and showed that generation quality tracks forward-pass compute, with DiT-XL/2 reaching an FID of 2.27 on class-conditional ImageNet 256 x 256 [3]. Almost all later video systems inherit either that backbone or the latent video pipeline built on top of latent diffusion models, which is why the following sections compare systems along four axes: what is tokenised, where the denoiser sits, how data and compute are scaled, and how the result is measured.

## Theme 1 — Tokenizers and backbones: three competing lineages

The first contrast is between architectures that predict **discrete tokens** and those that **denoise continuous latents**. VideoGPT [24] and, much later, Emu3 show the discrete route is still alive: Emu3 tokenises images, text and videos into one discrete space and trains a single transformer from scratch with next-token prediction only, reporting that it surpasses SDXL and LLaVA-1.6 and evaluating its video generation on VBench [9]. The continuous route split into two sub-lineages. The cascade lineage treats video as a sequence of super-resolution problems: Imagen Video's interleaved spatial and temporal super-resolution models with progressive distillation [2]. The single-pass lineage argues against it — Lumiere's Space-Time U-Net generates the whole temporal duration at once with spatial and temporal down- and up-sampling, and its authors state that keyframe-plus-temporal-super-resolution designs fail to resolve aliasing ambiguities consistently across temporal windows for globally coherent repetitive motion [25].

The second contrast is U-Net versus transformer. Latte converted the DiT idea into a video model by extracting spatio-temporal tokens and modelling the video distribution with Transformer blocks in latent space, adding four efficient variants obtained by decomposing the spatial and temporal dimensions because of the token count and reporting state-of-the-art results on FaceForensics, SkyTimelapse, UCF101 and Taichi-HD [26]. CogVideoX follows the same trajectory but pushes the tokenizer: it compresses video along both spatial and temporal dimensions with a 3D VAE and couples it to an "expert transformer" whose expert adaptive LayerNorm is designed to fuse text and video representations for better text-video alignment [4].

The third contrast concerns the tokenizer itself, which was long treated as fixed. ViTok scales the auto-encoder instead of the generator, replacing the convolutional backbone with an enhanced Vision Transformer and finding an asymmetry: scaling the encoder yields minimal gains for either reconstruction or generation, while scaling the decoder boosts reconstruction but gives mixed generation benefits [27]. Combined with Emu3's competing claim that one discrete token space suffices for text, image and video [9], this suggests the tokenizer — not only the denoiser — is now a design variable that the field has not settled.

## Theme 2 — Latent video diffusion at scale: data, staging and compute

Where Theme 1 compares architectures, this theme compares the training recipes that made them work at scale, and the recipes differ mainly in **staging**. Video LDM made the classic recipe explicit: pre-train a latent diffusion model on images only, then introduce a temporal dimension into the latent-space model and fine-tune it on encoded image sequences, additionally temporally aligning the diffusion upsamplers so that off-the-shelf image super-resolution models become temporally consistent video upsamplers; the resulting pipeline generated 512 x 1024 driving videos and, applied to public Stable Diffusion, text-to-video at up to 1280 x 2048, with the temporal layers shown to be reusable for the first personalised text-to-video results [28]. Stable Video Diffusion formalised this into three explicit stages — text-to-image pretraining, video pretraining, and high-quality video finetuning — and, more importantly, argued that the pretraining corpus itself must be curated, presenting captioning and filtering strategies as a first-class contribution; it also showed that the base model transfers to camera-motion-specific LoRA modules and to a feedforward multi-view diffusion model that outperformed image-based methods at a fraction of their compute [29]. CogVideoX adds a dedicated text-video data processing pipeline with a video captioning method reported to improve both generation quality and semantic alignment [4], and ViMix-14M frames the same problem as an access bottleneck: public corpora usually require manual crawl of video platforms, which yields low usable volume due to link rot and access limits and raises licensing uncertainty, motivating a curated crawl-free dataset of roughly 14 million pairs with long-form captions [30].

On the compute side the picture is similarly staged. Scaling Laws For Diffusion Transformers reports that DiT pretraining loss follows a power law with compute over budgets from 1e17 to 6e18 FLOPs, confirming scaling laws for diffusion transformers [31], which is what makes parameter-count decisions in the next section interpretable rather than empirical guesswork. HunyuanVideo, for instance, states that its scaling strategy reduced computational requirements by up to 5x while reaching the desired performance, with the 13B size chosen from scaling-law fits [6].

## Theme 3 — Recent large video generators: what converges and what diverges

Comparing 2024-2025 systems side by side, three things converge and three diverge.

**Converging.** (i) Scale: Wan 2.1 offers 1.3B and 14B models, the 14B trained on billions of images and videos, with the 1.3B reportedly needing only 8.19 GB of VRAM to run on consumer GPUs [5]; Movie Gen's largest video model is a 30B transformer trained with a 73K-token context that produces 16-second, 16 fps, 1080p video and is paired with a 13B audio model, pretrained jointly on O(100)M videos and O(1)B images [32]; HunyuanVideo's 13B is described as the largest among open-source models at release [6]. (ii) The latent-diffusion-plus-transformer recipe, now extended to audio: Veo 3 applies latent diffusion to temporal audio latents as well as spatio-temporal video latents, with captions annotated at several levels of detail by Gemini models [33]. (iii) Public benchmark reporting: Movie Gen released Movie Gen Video Bench of 1,003 prompts, which its authors claim is more than 3x larger than prompt sets used in prior work, plus non-cherry-picked generations for fair comparison [32], and Veo 3 evaluates on that same benchmark (1,003 video prompts and 527 video-and-audio prompts) as well as on the 355 image-text pair VBench I2V set [33].

**Diverging.** (i) Tokenizer compression as a speed lever rather than a quality lever: LTX-Video's Video-VAE reaches a 1:192 compression ratio with spatiotemporal downscaling of 32 x 32 x 8 pixels per token and 128 latent channels, producing 5 seconds of 24 fps 768 x 512 video in about 2 seconds on an H100 with fewer than 2B parameters [34]; Wan 2.2 instead uses a high-compression VAE with 4 x 16 x 16 compression and generates 5-second 720P video in under 9 minutes on a single consumer-grade GPU [7]. (ii) Dense versus sparse capacity: Wan 2.2 introduces a mixture-of-experts design in which a high-noise expert handles early-stage layout and a low-noise expert refines details, each about 14B parameters for 27B total but only 14B active per step, chosen by an SNR threshold, and trained with +65.6% more images and +83.2% more videos than Wan 2.1 [7]. Mochi 1 goes the opposite way, with a single 10B asymmetric diffusion transformer whose visual stream has nearly four times as many parameters as its text stream and a single T5-XXL prompt encoder, released under Apache 2.0 but limited to 480p and, by its own model card, weak on animated content and prone to warping under extreme motion [35]. (iii) Evaluation philosophy: Wan 2.1 introduces Wan-Bench, an automated benchmark with 14 fine-grained metrics motivated by the claim that FVD and FID "lack alignment with human perception" [5], whereas HunyuanVideo reports a human study with 1,533 prompts, one generation per model to avoid cherry-picking, and 60 professional evaluators, scoring 41.3% overall against 27.4% for GEN-3 alpha and 24.8% for Luma1.6 [6]; CogVideoX reports a 100-prompt human evaluation on four axes in which CogVideoX-5B scores 2.74 against 2.17 for Kling, along with inference costs such as 113 seconds and 26 GB for 5 seconds of 480 x 720 video [36]. These numbers are not comparable across papers because prompts, raters and axes differ — which is precisely the gap the next section discusses.

## Theme 4 — Unified multimodal generation: from image+text to any-to-any

If video systems converged on one backbone, multimodal generation has not converged on one objective. Four design families appear in the recent literature, and the empirical disagreement between them is explicit.

**Family A — pure autoregression over discrete tokens.** Chameleon is an early-fusion family trained from scratch on interleaved images, text and code that quantises images into discrete tokens analogous to words, so the same transformer handles both modalities without separate encoders or domain-specific decoders; it reports state-of-the-art image captioning, competitiveness with Mixtral 8x7B and Gemini-Pro on text tasks, and human-judged parity with Gemini Pro and GPT-4V on long-form mixed-modal generation [8]. Emu3 extends the same idea to video, tokenising images, text and videos into a discrete space and using only next-token prediction, explicitly positioned against diffusion models and CLIP-plus-LLM compositions [9].

**Family B — hybrid next-token plus diffusion.** Transfusion trains a single transformer on mixed-modality sequences with a language-modelling loss on text and a diffusion loss on images, and reports that it "scales significantly better than quantizing images and training a language model over discrete image tokens"; pretrained up to 7B parameters on 2T multimodal tokens, it matches comparable-scale diffusion and language models, and can compress each image to 16 patches [10]. This is a direct empirical contradiction of Family A's premise. Show-o takes a closely related route, unifying autoregressive and discrete diffusion modelling in one transformer that supports VQA, text-to-image generation, inpainting and extrapolation, and motivates itself against assembled systems such as NExT-GPT, which needs an additional pretrained diffusion model for image generation [11].

**Family C — autoregressive backbones with continuous-token generative heads.** NextStep-1 pairs a 14B autoregressive model with a 157M flow-matching head over continuous image tokens, avoiding both the compute of running heavy diffusion models over continuous tokens and the quantisation loss of vector quantisation, and argues that a performance gap between autoregressive and state-of-the-art diffusion image models still had to be closed [12]. At the architecture level, the MMDiT design introduced with Stable Diffusion 3 uses separate weights for text and image tokens with a bidirectional information flow between them, follows predictable scaling trends, and later serves as the reusable visual-stream block of unified systems [13].

**Family D — shared diffusion or flow spaces over many modalities.** NExT-OMNI replaces autoregression with discrete flow matching over a shared space and is trained on interleaved text, image, video and audio, claiming native any-to-any understanding and generation; it rejects both purely autoregressive designs, which it says prevent a balanced integration of understanding and generation, and hybrid or decoupled designs, which it calls redundant and non-integrated [14]. Omni-Diffusion applies masked discrete diffusion to text, speech and images through a three-stage progressive training pipeline, but does not claim video generation [15]. Dynin-Omni is also masked-diffusion based, unifies text, image and speech generation with video understanding, and reports 87.6 on GSM8K, 61.4 on VideoMME and 0.87 on GenEval across 19 benchmarks [17]. AR-Omni pushes back on the need for extra components at all, using a single token stream, one next-token objective and a single decoder with no expert decoders, and fixes modality imbalance with task-aware loss reweighting plus a token-level perceptual alignment loss for image tokens, reporting a 0.88 real-time factor for streaming speech [16]. MUNITE avoids choosing a head entirely by treating encoding and latent generation as the same inference problem under different amounts of observed evidence, so that a full observation recovers deterministic encoding and no observation recovers the latent marginal inside one conditional flow model [37].

The net reading is that modality coverage, not raw quality, is what separates these systems today: several members of Families A-C generate only text and images [8][11][13][12], Emu3 and NExT-OMNI add video [9][14], NExT-OMNI is the only retrieved model claiming audio and video generation together [14], and the masked-diffusion omni models still treat video as an understanding task only [17]. Video is therefore the modality that tests unification, because it is where the cost of a shared token space (long token sequences) and the cost of a separate expert denoiser (no joint training) are both highest.

## Theme 5 — Evaluation, controllability and efficiency

**Evaluation has moved from fidelity to faithfulness, without converging.** VBench-2.0 targets "intrinsic faithfulness" across five categories and 18 fine-grained dimensions (Human Fidelity, Controllability, Creativity, Physics, Commonsense), combining generalist VLM/LLM judges with specialist anomaly detectors and human preference annotations, and reports per-dimension results in which Sora reaches 94.15% on Instance Preservation while CogVideoX-1.5 reaches 82.46% [19]. Earlier suites are narrower: VBench's 16 dimensions split into video quality and video-condition consistency with 100 prompts per dimension, EvalCrafter's 700 prompts over 10 text-to-video models, and T2VSafetyBench's 5,151 malicious prompts across 9 models [18]. Public leaderboards have converged on pairwise human preference instead: the Artificial Analysis Video Arena derives Elo ratings from blind pairwise votes aggregated with Bradley-Terry maximum likelihood estimation, and deliberately separates silent from audio-enabled generation and editing from generation, so the boards are not mutually comparable [18]. Against this, several flagship model reports still lead with internal studies on their own prompt sets [5][6], which is why cross-paper numbers such as 41.3% for HunyuanVideo [6] or 2.74 for CogVideoX-5B [36] cannot be read as a shared ranking.

**Physics and authenticity are the sharpest measured failures.** Physics-IQ scores generated videos against physical principles on a scale whose maximum of 100.0% is achievable only by videos that adhere to all of its tested principles; verified-leaderboard entries retrieved for this survey include Magi-1 24B with GeoPhys and best-of-N at 58.2%, Hunyuan Video 1.5 (image-to-video) at 33.4% and Wan 2.2 (image-to-video) at 32.2%, while the earlier original leaderboard lists Sora at 10.0%, Stable Video Diffusion at 14.8% and Lumiere in the 19.0-23.0% range [20]. A survey of AI-generated video evaluation argues that the field's fundamental limitation is the divergence between high visual realism and physical plausibility, cites benchmarks showing violations of momentum conservation and rigid-body collisions, and documents an evaluation landscape fragmented across visual fidelity, temporal consistency, semantic alignment and physical validity [38]. The same survey collects safety results that mirror this fragility: concept erasure reduces undesirable content by 46% on average, DPO-based safety alignment improves safety by up to 42.39%, watermarking embeds 48 bits per frame with 0.96 tamper-localisation accuracy, single-word prompt edits substantially degrade semantic and temporal quality in ModelScope, CogVideoX, Open-Sora and HunyuanVideo, and multi-turn attack strategies approach 100% success rates against models with perfect single-turn refusal [38]. Detection benchmarks fare no better: AEGIS collects over 10,000 real and synthetic videos drawn from models including Stable Video Diffusion, CogVideoX-5B, KLing and Sora with GPT-4o-refined hard subsets, and reports that advanced vision-language models show limited detection capability on the most challenging subsets [39].

**Controllability and efficiency are the two engineering bottlenecks.** A survey of controllable video generation starts from the observation that text prompts alone are insufficient for complex, multi-modal and fine-grained requirements, and organises control into seven classes (structure, identity, image, temporal — flow, trajectory, camera, motion — audio, other, and universal multi-condition control) after screening 708 papers and reviewing 388 full texts with 0.87 inter-rater agreement on 100 papers; it names temporally consistent depth from monocular RGB and the consistent fusion of multiple interacting conditions as unresolved [40]. On cost, a survey of efficient video diffusion models groups methods into step distillation, efficient attention, model compression and cache/trajectory optimisation, reports that video-only acceleration work remains a minority of the acceleration literature relative to image-only work, and argues video must be treated as an independent efficiency problem because of temporal coherence constraints, long-context memory growth and cross-step error accumulation; within step distillation it singles out distribution distillation as the only approach that has reliably entered the one-to-four-step regime while warning that its weakness is fragility rather than expressivity [21]. Systems papers attack the same cost from the attention side: MC-Sparse reports a training-free framework that selects key-value tokens and reuses cached metadata across denoising steps for a 1.80x denoising speedup on video and 2.32x on 3D assets with negligible quality loss [41]. Long-horizon generation remains a distinct problem, because whole-frame metrics conflate desirable motion with background drift — SNF-Bench separates static support from dynamic flow to report static fidelity and flow persistence with absolute magnitude [23] — and because memory mechanisms are caught between unbounded storage, as in full-history retention, and one-time retention decisions that go stale as the generated history evolves [22].

## Trends and open problems

**Trends.** First, architecture consolidation with a twist: latent diffusion and flow-matching Transformers are the default [3][26][4][33], but the new competition is at the edges of the pipeline — tokenizer compression [27][34], mixture-of-experts capacity [7] and audio latents inside the same diffusion process [33] rather than a new denoising objective. Second, the centre of gravity is shifting from single-modality video models to unified systems, and the field currently splits on the representation question: continuous latents with diffusion or flow heads are argued to scale better [10][12], while discrete-token autoregression is argued to be sufficient and simpler [8][9], with masked and discrete diffusion offering a third shared space [14][15][17]. Third, evaluation is being rebuilt around faithfulness, physics and authenticity rather than FVD-like fidelity [19][38][20], in parallel with leaderboards built on blind pairwise preference [18] — a methodological shift as much as a metric shift. Fourth, acceleration research is itself becoming a subfield, with video-specific work growing fast but still a minority of the literature [21] and sparse attention presented as the main lever for long-sequence denoising [41].

**Open problems.** (i) Long-horizon consistency: streaming introduces exposure bias, history drift and KV-cache growth, and every memory design trades storage cost against staleness [21][22], while evaluation of fixed-camera drift is only now being separated from genuine motion [23]. (ii) Physical validity: verified Physics-IQ entries for recent systems still sit far below the benchmark's 100% ceiling [20], and the realism-versus-plausibility gap is described as a fundamental limitation rather than an engineering detail [38]. (iii) Controllability under multiple interacting conditions, especially temporally consistent depth and the fusion of several conditions in one sequence [40]. (iv) Data: open text-to-video remains bottlenecked by corpora that are expensive to obtain and licensing-uncertain [30]. (v) Evaluation and safety infrastructure: the evaluation landscape is fragmented across four quality dimensions with no unified protocol [38], authenticity detection generalises poorly to high-fidelity fakes [39], and safety attacks succeed with trivial word-level edits or multi-turn strategies [38]. (vi) Modality coverage: unified models still mostly generate text and images, with video and audio generation the least covered [14][17], so any-to-any remains a target rather than a solved setting.
