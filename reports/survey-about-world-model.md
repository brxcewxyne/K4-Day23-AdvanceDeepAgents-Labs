# World Models: Architectures, Generative Simulators, and How We Measure Them

## TL;DR

- A "world model" is a learned internal predictive representation of how an environment evolves, used to predict, plan and reason: one widely cited survey defines world models as "internal simulators that learn the structure and dynamics of an environment" [1], and the same definitional debate splits the field into "understanding the world" versus "predicting the future" [2].
- Two lineages dominate. Latent state-space agents learn a compact dynamics model and train policies inside it — PlaNet's deterministic-plus-stochastic latent dynamics [3], Dreamer's latent imagination [4], and DreamerV3, the first algorithm to collect diamonds in Minecraft from scratch without human data or curricula with a single configuration [5][6] — while the MuZero line predicts only decision-relevant quantities (reward, policy, value) rather than observations [7][8].
- Since 2023 the field has shifted to large generative video models as simulators: Genie, an 11B-parameter model trained unsupervised on unlabelled internet video with an inferred latent action space [9], Genie 2/3 as interactive, real-time 3D world generators [10][11][12], and open, real-time game engines such as Matrix-Game 2.0 at 25 FPS [13], RELIC at 16 FPS with a 14B model [14] and WanToFight at 30 FPS [15].
- Evaluation has moved from visual fidelity to functional utility, and the two do not track each other: WorldArena reports a "significant perception–functionality gap" across 14 models [16], WBench finds no single model strong across all five evaluation dimensions among 20 state-of-the-art models [17], and WMBench concludes evaluator quality is dominated by long-horizon action-faithful rollout consistency rather than short-term visual realism [18].
- The unresolved problems are consistent across surveys: long-horizon consistency and error compounding, action-conditioned controllability, physical consistency versus pixel fidelity, real-time efficiency, and a definition of "world model" that remains contested [19][20][21][22].

## Background

The modern use of the term comes from Ha and Schmidhuber's *Recurrent World Models*, which trains "a large RNN that learns to predict the future given the past in an unsupervised manner" and separates the spatial representation of observations from their temporal dynamics; a small controller is then trained by evolution, and notably "entirely inside of an environment generated by its own internal world model" before transferring back [23]. PlaNet generalised this into learning dynamics from pixels and choosing actions by fast online planning in latent space with "a latent dynamics model with both deterministic and stochastic transition components" — the ancestor of the RSSM family — trained with a multi-step variational objective the authors name latent overshooting [3]. Dreamer then showed that behaviours can be learned by propagating analytic gradients of learned state values back through trajectories imagined in the compact state space of the learned model, which is what makes imagining thousands of trajectories in parallel affordable [4].

Two framings of the "model" coexist from the start. The reconstruction-and-imagination line models observations or their latents; the MuZero line instead learns "a model that, when applied iteratively, predicts the quantities most directly relevant to planning: the reward, the action-selection policy, and the value function", achieving superhuman Go, chess and shogi without knowledge of the game rules and a new state of the art on 57 Atari games [7]. A third, non-generative framing comes from LeCun's position paper: a JEPA "predicts the representation s_y from s_x rather than reconstructing y", on the argument that it is essentially impossible to predict every pixel of every future frame, so unpredictable detail should be ignored in latent space rather than generated [24]. I-JEPA instantiates this for images, predicting the representations of target blocks from a single context block without hand-crafted augmentations [25]. Model-based RL surveys provide the surrounding theory and the vocabulary the field borrows: model-based RL is any MDP approach that uses a model and learns a global value or policy [26], and for non-tabular environments there is "always a generalization error between the learned environment model and the real environment", a gap that motivates model-uncertainty handling [27]. The same literature usefully separates three regimes that the world-model debate tends to blur: planning with a known model, planning over a learned model without learning a global solution, and learning a model together with a global value or policy [26] — which is roughly the distinction between a simulator, a search-based agent, and an imagination-trained agent.

## From latent imagination to scalable agents

The Dreamer line is the clearest evidence that a learned latent model can substitute for the environment. DreamerV2 uses discrete latent representations in a world model trained separately from the policy and is described as "the first agent that achieves human-level performance on the Atari benchmark of 55 tasks by learning behaviors inside a separately trained world model" [28]. DreamerV3 keeps one configuration and "outperforms specialized methods across over 150 diverse tasks", spanning Atari, ProcGen, DMLab, robot locomotion and manipulation, and it is the first algorithm to collect diamonds in Minecraft from scratch without human data or curricula [5]; the Nature version states the same headline claim, reports non-visual domains alongside the visual ones, and notes that while several strong baselines progress only to advanced items such as the iron pickaxe, Dreamer "is the only compared algorithm that discovers diamonds, and does so in every training run" [6]. Dreamer 4 extends this to offline data only, hitting real-time interactive inference on a single GPU through a shortcut forcing objective and an efficient transformer, with general action conditioning learned from a small amount of labelled data and the majority of knowledge taken from diverse unlabelled videos [29].

Two things distinguish these from the video-generation camp. First, the model is trained jointly with a control objective and evaluated by task return; the Crafter leaderboard illustrates how hard the resulting benchmark is, with DreamerV3 at 14.5±1.6 against an expert-human score of 50.5% on the geometric mean of 22 achievements [30]. Second, alternative backbones and objectives are being explored: S4WM is the first world model compatible with parallelisable state-space models like S4 and, incorporating latent variable modelling, beats Transformer-based world models on long-term memory while being more efficient in training and imagination [31]. Architecturally, TD-MPC2 shows the opposite trade-off — local trajectory optimisation in the latent space of a learned *implicit*, decoder-free world model — improving over baselines across 104 online RL tasks with a single hyperparameter set and scaling to a 317M-parameter multi-task agent that reaches 70.63 on an 80-task normalized score versus 15.98 for a 1M-parameter agent [32]. Sample efficiency in the discrete-action regime is dominated by the MuZero line: EfficientZero, built on MuZero, adds self-supervised temporally consistent model learning, end-to-end value prefix learning to alleviate compounding error, and learned-model correction of off-policy value targets, reaching 194.3% mean and 109.0% median human performance on Atari 100k with roughly two hours of game experience [8]. DIAMOND pushes in the opposite direction, arguing that compressing the world model into discrete latents discards visual detail that matters, and trains an agent inside a diffusion world model to a mean human-normalized score of 1.46 on Atari 100k — a new best for agents trained entirely within a world model [33].

## Predictive representations, not pixels

The JEPA family treats the world model as a predictor in representation space. I-JEPA predicts the representations of target blocks from a context block in the same image, with masking chosen so target blocks are semantically large and the context block is spatially distributed [25]. V-JEPA 2 scales this to video: an action-free JEPA pre-trained on over one million hours of internet video, which yields 77.3 top-1 on Something-Something v2 and 39.7 recall-at-5 on Epic-Kitchens-100, and reaches 84.0 on PerceptionTest and 76.9 on TempCompass after alignment with an LLM at 8B scale; a latent action-conditioned world model, V-JEPA 2-AC, is then post-trained on less than 62 hours of unlabelled Droid robot video for planning [34].

This branch is also the most contested. LeCun's case for non-generative prediction is explicitly a critique of pixel-level objectives [24]. *Critiques of World Models* attacks it from the other side: it claims JEPA remains autoregressive and generative in the functional sense of recursively simulating latent states, that latent-only supervision makes it hard to diagnose meaningful dynamics versus collapse to trivial solutions, and that evidence of practical usability is scarce and mostly confined to toy environments [22]. A philosophical critique of self-supervised video learning goes further, arguing that systems such as V-JEPA 2 "cannot achieve understanding in any philosophically robust sense" and that the failure is architectural rather than empirical, while conceding legitimate applications such as warehouse robotics and video compression [35]. These critiques are not settled by benchmark scores, and any survey of the field has to present them as an open, largely conceptual dispute.

## Generative video models as simulators

The generative branch abandons the compact latent state for a video model that can be controlled. Genie is the canonical statement: an 11B-parameter model trained unsupervised from unlabelled internet videos, composed of a spatiotemporal video tokenizer, an autoregressive dynamics model and a "simple and scalable latent action model", letting users act frame-by-frame despite never seeing ground-truth action labels [9]. Genie 2 is an autoregressive latent diffusion model trained on a large video dataset that turns a single prompt image into an action-controllable, playable 3D environment, with object interactions, character animation and physics described as emergent at scale [10]; Genie 3 is described as the first world model to allow real-time interaction while improving consistency and realism, but its memory "recall[s] changes from specific interactions for up to a minute" [11]. Project Genie is the productised prototype, and its own disclosure lists remaining limitations in world realism and character control [12].

Several groups attack the same problem with different conditioning interfaces and speed targets. GameNGen is a diffusion model trained on recorded DOOM gameplay that runs at 20 frames per second on a single TPU, stays stable over multi-minute sessions, and reaches PSNR 29.4 for next-frame prediction, with human raters only slightly better than chance at telling short clips apart from the real game [36]. Oasis is a 500M-parameter spatial autoencoder plus latent diffusion transformer trained with diffusion forcing, generating real time at 20 FPS, and states plainly that "there is no physics engine; just a foundation model" while noting that a further order of magnitude of speed needs new hardware [37]. Matrix-Game scales interactive game world generation to over 17 billion parameters and introduces the GameWorld Score benchmark covering visual quality, temporal quality, action controllability and physical rule understanding, claiming to beat Oasis and MineWorld across all metrics, especially controllability and physical consistency [38]. Matrix-Game 2.0 addresses latency directly by replacing bidirectional attention with a causal, distilled architecture over roughly 1,200 hours of Unreal Engine and GTA5 footage, reaching 25 FPS on minute-level videos [13]. RELIC uses a 14B model with compressed historical latent tokens carrying relative actions and absolute camera poses in the KV cache to achieve memory-aware real-time exploration at 16 FPS [14], and WanToFight sustains 30 FPS at 512x384 on a single RTX 5090 for two-player fighting-game interaction [15]. Cheaper recipes exist too: minWM converts existing bidirectional text-to-video foundation models into camera-controllable few-step autoregressive world models through causal forcing and distillation [39], GameFactory targets scene generalisation with pre-trained video diffusion and a multi-phase training strategy [40], and MineWorld uses a visual-action autoregressive Transformer rather than diffusion [41]. The ambitions of this branch are stated most explicitly in a position paper that proposes interactive generative video as the foundation of generative game engines capable of unlimited novel content and lays out an L0-L4 maturity roadmap [42].

Domain-specialised versions make the conditioning requirements explicit. GAIA-1 casts driving world modelling as unsupervised next-token prediction over video, text and action inputs, allowing rollouts conditioned on speed and curvature or on text [43]; GAIA-2 is a latent diffusion world model producing multi-camera, spatiotemporally consistent driving video conditioned on ego-vehicle dynamics, agent configurations, environmental factors and road semantics, motivated by the failure of existing approaches on multi-agent interaction, fine-grained control and multi-camera consistency [44]. Navigation World Models predict future visual observations from past observations and navigation actions with a Conditional Diffusion Transformer scaled to 1B parameters, requiring 4x fewer FLOPs than a standard DiT [45]. UniSim learns a universal simulator by orchestrating datasets rich along different dimensions, simulates both high-level instructions and low-level controls, and trains policies that deploy zero-shot [46]. Pandora couples a 7B LLM with a pretrained video model for free-text action control [47], WorldDreamer frames world modelling as masked visual token prediction [48], and the Cosmos World Foundation Model platform packages a video curation pipeline, pre-trained world foundation models and video tokenizers as a general-purpose base that can be post-trained for camera control, robotic manipulation and driving [49].

The most useful cross-cutting observation from this branch comes from its surveys. One organises interactive world modelling by application scenario, world-state evolution and scene modality, and names three crucial challenges: action-conditioned controllability, long-horizon interaction and memory, and action-following responsiveness for real-time interactivity [50]. Another rethinks interactive world models as game engines along player action control, game state dynamics, state-observation persistence and real-time generation, and argues that a genuine game engine needs interaction outcomes that follow rules over evolving conditions, consequences that persist over long horizons, and a real-time loop — properties conventional engines get from an explicit recurrent state loop, and which video models currently only approximate [51]. A third treats video generators as learning the transition function P(s_{t+1} | s_t, a_t) and shows that efficiency is a prerequisite for the paradigm: distillation cuts denoising from tens of steps to a few or one, yet does not resolve persistent rollout error, and over a 10-minute generation window most methods struggle with object permanence and structural consistency [21].

## Embodied agents, robotics and the uses of a world model

For embodied systems, a world model is justified by what it is used *for*. The robot-learning survey enumerates the roles directly — policy learning, planning, simulation, evaluation and data generation — and traces robotic video world models from imagination-based generation to controllable, structured and foundation-scale formulations [52]. Concretely: DayDreamer applies Dreamer to four robots with the same hyperparameters, training a quadruped to roll over, stand up and walk from scratch without resets in one hour and adapting to perturbations within ten minutes, where SAC learns only to roll over [53]. UniPi casts sequential decision making as text-conditioned video generation and extracts actions by inverse dynamics, reporting that a success classifier on generated final frames improves from 72.6% trained from scratch to 77.1% finetuned from an internet-pretrained model, while cautioning that CLIPScore does not reflect task failure and that better control-specific generation metrics are needed [54]. RoboDreamer factorises video generation by parsing instructions into primitives, which additionally allows language-plus-goal-image conditioning and outperforms monolithic video generators, while admitting it generalises poorly to many real-world images because robotics datasets lack diversity [55]. Navigation World Models plan by simulating trajectories and scoring them against a goal image under an MPC formulation, and can incorporate constraints such as forbidding left turns that a fixed policy cannot [45]. MineDojo supplies the open-ended evaluation substrate — thousands of language-prompted tasks, 730K YouTube videos and a video-language metric that doubles as reward and automatic scorer [56].

A newer and arguably more consequential use is *evaluation*. WorldGym uses an autoregressive action-conditioned video model as a proxy environment, evaluating VLA policies by Monte Carlo rollouts with a vision-language model providing rewards, and reports that success rates inside the world model correlate highly with real-world success and that relative policy rankings are preserved across policy versions, sizes and checkpoints [57]. GigaWorld-1's WMBench pushes this to a controlled study: 7 video world models, 4 action representation schemes, over 324,000 simulated policy rollouts paired with real robot executions and more than 12,000 hours of data, with the central finding that evaluator quality is dominated by long-horizon, action-faithful rollout consistency rather than short-term visual realism [18]. Video models are also being used as evaluators without any explicit world-model training, as in a generative evaluation system built on Veo for assessing robot policies on nominal performance, out-of-distribution generalisation and safety [58]. The caveat is that the evidence for downstream utility is still mostly correlational and benchmark-specific: WorldGym reports qualitative claims without correlation coefficients, and GigaWorld-1's conclusions come from one benchmark construction [57][18].

## Competing taxonomies and the definitional dispute

Because the term is contested, the surveys themselves are part of the object of study. The most cited general survey splits world models by function — constructing internal representations to understand the world versus predicting future states to simulate and guide decisions — and criticises domain-specific surveys for failing to examine what precisely constitutes a world model, while listing restricted causal reasoning and inconsistent reproduction of physical laws as limitations of leading video models [2]. A 2026 survey proposes a four-axis taxonomy over architecture, methodological family, reasoning strategy and application domain, and traces the historical line from PlaNet, the Dreamer family and MuZero to Sora, Cosmos and Genie [1]. For embodied AI, a three-axis taxonomy crosses functionality, temporal modelling and spatial representation, and systematises datasets and metrics across robotics, driving and general video [19]. A capability hierarchy frames progress as plausible, controllable and actionable models, arguing that value lies in improving behaviour rather than visual fidelity, and crosses geometry, physics and action grounding with improvement loops over data, rewards, policies and the model [59]. A position-driven survey proposes L1 Predictor, L2 Simulator and L3 Evolver levels crossed with physical, digital, social and scientific law regimes, explicitly criticising modality- or application-based partitions as unable to capture capability progression across modalities [20]. A 3D/4D survey complains that the absence of a standardised definition and taxonomy has produced fragmented and inconsistent claims, and separates generative from predictive paradigms over geometric, action-based and semantic conditioning [60]. Domain surveys do the same for driving, with a three-tier taxonomy over future-world generation, behaviour planning and the interaction between prediction and planning [61]. A mechanistic view of video generation as world modelling organises the field by state construction (implicit context management versus explicit latent compression) and dynamics modelling (causal knowledge integration versus causal architecture reformulation), and argues evaluation must move from visual fidelity to functional benchmarks testing physical persistence and causal reasoning [62].

The sharpest disagreement is whether video generation counts at all. *Critiques of World Models* argues the primary goal is "simulating all actionable possibilities of the real world for purposeful reasoning and acting", that Sora- and Veo-style models generate fixed trajectories without explicit state, action or object-level representation and therefore "fall outside the definition of world models for reasoning and planning", and proposes a Generative Latent Prediction architecture as an alternative [22]. Against that, the practical line of work treats controllability as the operative test, and multiple interactive benchmarks now measure exactly that. Meanwhile, an industry landscape report — explicitly not peer reviewed — proposes five axes (domain, function, representation, time horizon, action conditioning), six model families, and argues that visual realism is not sufficient and that universal rankings should be treated with caution because no single benchmark covers the space [63]. Read together, the taxonomies agree on the axes that matter (state, dynamics, action conditioning, horizon, representation) far more than they agree on membership.

## How world models are evaluated

Benchmarks have proliferated along three roughly separate tracks. The first scores video generation quality: WorldModelBench adds instruction-following and a five-law physical-adherence dimension scored binary over 350 prompts, crowd-sources 67K human labels over 14 frontier models, and fine-tunes a 2B judger that beats GPT-4o by 8.6% average accuracy; it also reports a 0.69 correlation with VBench on frame-wise quality but only 0.28 on physics adherence, concluding VBench does not distinguish videos by physical-law adherence [64]. WorldScore decomposes world generation into next-scene tasks with camera-trajectory layouts over 3,000 test examples and 10 metrics, evaluating 20 models including open and closed video and 3D scene generators [65]. WorldSimBench proposes a dual framework of explicit perceptual evaluation and implicit manipulative evaluation across open-ended embodied environments, driving and manipulation, including a human-preference evaluator trained on a fine-grained feedback dataset and an action-consistency test of whether generated video can be translated into correct control signals [66].

The second track scores embodied functionality. WorldArena measures video perception quality with 16 metrics over six sub-dimensions and separately evaluates world models as data engines, policy evaluators and action planners, aggregating with the EWMScore index; across 14 models it finds a significant perception–functionality gap, and human annotators rated 3,500 videos on quality, instruction following and physical adherence, with action-conditioned models such as CtrlWorld showing notably better physical adherence than text-only counterparts [16]. Its 2.0 extension widens the evaluation to multimodal perception, interactive RL environments and diverse robotic platforms beyond vision-only simulators [67]. EWMBench evaluates scene consistency, motion correctness and semantic alignment on 30 samples across 10 tasks, generating 2,100 videos from 7 generators, and reports that domain-adapted models consistently beat commercial and open-source general ones and that its rankings align more closely with human judgments than VBench's [68]. WBench is multi-turn: 289 test cases and 1,058 interaction turns over five dimensions with 22 automatic sub-metrics, and its headline finding is that no single one of 20 state-of-the-art models performs strongly across all dimensions [17]. WorldMark attacks comparability directly, providing a shared WASD-style action vocabulary mapped into each model's native control format to evaluate six models on identical scenes and trajectories over 500 cases, plus an online arena [69]. PAWBench pushes evaluation to the distribution level, asking whether repeated rollouts recover the correct distribution of behaviours under the same observation and action, and finds across 50 scenarios and eleven systems that no model consistently matches the reference probabilities while recovering the range of valid behaviours [70].

The third track is classical agent evaluation, which remains the ground truth for control claims: Atari 100k and 200M protocols [6][8][33], DeepMind Control and Meta-World/ManiSkill2/MyoSuite for continuous control [32], Crafter's 22-achievement geometric mean [30], DMLab and ProcGen [5][6], and Minecraft diamond collection as a long-horizon milestone [5][6][29]. The practical weakness is re-reporting: Atari 100k numbers circulate through third-party reimplementation pages that report a 120% human-normalized mean for a PyTorch reimplementation against 112% for the official DreamerV3, with per-game deltas such as Breakout +110% and Chopper Command +168% [71]. Such pages are useful as evidence of how results are cross-checked in practice, but they are not the papers themselves.

## Trends and open problems

Three shifts are visible. First, the centre of gravity moved from latent state-space models evaluated on task return to large generative video models evaluated on perceptual and interactive quality, and the field is now trying to pull the two back together — the Dreamer line adds scalability and transformers [29], while the generative line adds causal, distilled, real-time architectures [13][39]. Second, "interactive" became the operative word: latent action inference without labels [9], real-time frame generation [11][36][37], memory over minutes [11][14], and multi-player adversarial control [15]. Third, evaluation migrated from fidelity to function, with policy-evaluation world models [57][18][58] and benchmarks that deliberately measure the gap between looking right and being useful [16][17].

The open problems are, encouragingly, the same list in almost every source. Long-horizon consistency and error compounding is the most frequently named: Dreamer 4 motivates itself by prior models' inability to predict object interactions accurately [29], LIVE uses cycle consistency to control error accumulation beyond the training horizon because standard autoregressive rollouts accumulate small errors [72], and the efficiency survey reports object permanence and structural consistency degrading within a 10-minute window for most methods [21]. Action-conditioned controllability and data cost come next — interventional data is expensive and the field acknowledges that exploratory actions in the real world may cause damage [49][50]. Physical consistency remains only loosely related to pixel realism [64][16]. Real-time efficiency is a hard constraint for interaction rather than a nicety [37][21]. Evaluation is fragmented and unstable, with different benchmarks producing different rankings and no single benchmark covering the space [63][19]. Memory remains bounded [11], uncertainty calibration and causal intervention testing are largely unaddressed [59], and the definitional question — whether a video model that produces fixed trajectories without explicit state and action can be called a world model — is argued rather than measured [22][62][35]. The most honest summary is that the field has converged on what a world model should do (predict how the world evolves under intervention, over long horizons, at interactive speed) far more than on how to prove that a given model does it.
